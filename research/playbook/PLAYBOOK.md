# LinkedIn Organic Content Strategy Playbook — B2B SaaS

I built this from the 10 experts I researched (`research/sources.md`, `research/linkedin-posts/`, `research/youtube-transcripts/`). Every recommendation below is cited back to the specific post or video it came from.

LinkedIn post citations include a direct link and an exact date, pulled via a LinkedIn post date-extractor tool. YouTube citations are sourced by video title and speaker rather than a direct link.

---

## 1. Core Recommendations

**Write the first three lines like they're the whole post, not a teaser for it.**
LinkedIn only shows three lines before the "see more" cutoff. Jasmin Alic's structure is a one-line hook, a blank line, then a second line, and together those three lines should carry the entire point of the post (source: Jasmin Alic, "LinkedIn Growth Strategy: Secret Playbook Revealed," YouTube). Matt Lakajev uses the exact same one-line-plus-gap structure and gives the same reason for it (source: Matt Lakajev, "How I got 20,000 views and 13 leads from 1 LinkedIn post," YouTube). Two people who don't know each other landing on the same mechanic independently is a good sign it's real, not a trend.

**Default to text-only. Skip image and video unless there's a specific reason not to.**
Jasmin Alic's own account data shows text-only posts consistently outreach his image posts (source: Jasmin Alic, "2025 LinkedIn Strategies That Actually Work," YouTube). Guillaume Moubeche made the same call running a B2B company page — he pulled video and leaned into plain text with an actual voice instead (source: Guillaume Moubeche, "Grow your LinkedIn Business Page — My #1 Strategy," YouTube).

**Track a ratio, not a raw number, to know if a post is actually working.**
Samantha McKenna's "Dark Social Score" is impressions divided by likes. A high score means people are screenshotting your post and forwarding it into Slack or DMs instead of engaging on-platform, which is the point if the goal is brand awareness, not vanity metrics (source: Samantha McKenna, LinkedIn post, 17.06.2026, https://www.linkedin.com/posts/samsalesli_samsales-lieb-ugcPost-7472733284138131457-6xwl/). This tracks with Richard van der Blom's data that saves, not likes, are the real reach signal — 10+ saves correlates to a 2,085% reach lift (source: Richard van der Blom, LinkedIn post, 25.06.2026, https://www.linkedin.com/posts/richardvanderblom_linkedin-just-made-a-small-but-smart-change-share-7475847202792378368-JTMU/).

**Build content people will actually forward, not just click.**
Amanda Natividad's point on "forwardable" content: a benchmark report or a teardown post is something a VP can drop straight into a Slack thread. A generic "5 tips" post isn't (source: Amanda Natividad, LinkedIn post, 13.05.2026, https://www.linkedin.com/posts/amandanat_b2b-buyers-are-on-linkedin-is-true-its-share-7460212223936724992-q8zt/). She backs this with a separate framework, Content-as-a-Service, where every piece of content gets a "client" (the internal team it serves), a "job" (the outcome it drives), and a metric (source: Amanda Natividad, LinkedIn post, 07.10.2025, https://www.linkedin.com/posts/amandanat_stop-treating-content-like-a-vending-machine-ugcPost-7381214423559888897-oVQF/).

**End on a question that takes five seconds to answer, not one that makes someone think.**
Jasmin Alic's data shows specific, yes/no, or single-metric questions convert way more readers into commenters than open-ended prompts that ask someone to recall a story (source: Jasmin Alic, "2025 LinkedIn Strategies That Actually Work," YouTube).

**Prove you actually know what you're talking about inside the post, not just at the end.**
Jasmin Alic calls this "sign-posting" — one half-sentence naming your specific experience, dropped into the post, so it doesn't read like something AI generated or something copy-pasted from someone else's feed (source: Jasmin Alic, "2025 LinkedIn Strategies That Actually Work," YouTube).

**On case study posts, cut the industry name out of the hook.**
If the first three lines say "aviation," everyone who isn't in aviation scrolls past before they even hit "see more." Jasmin Alic keeps the hook built around the underlying problem, industry-agnostic, and only names the specific client after the click (source: Jasmin Alic, "2025 LinkedIn Strategies That Actually Work," YouTube).

**The post gets the first look. The DM is where trust actually gets built.**
Matt Lakajev's own funnel numbers: 79% of his booked calls happened only after 40+ DMs of back-and-forth. Content is the awareness layer. It's not the thing that closes anyone (source: Matt Lakajev, LinkedIn post, 17.06.2025, https://www.linkedin.com/posts/mattlakajev_if-youre-trying-to-sell-on-linkedin-you-share-7340686459739193345-V8RT/).

**If you're going to cite a stat, make it specific enough to sound real.**
A flat "30%" reads like marketing copy and people discount it on sight. Josh Braun's point is to anchor claims to a slightly odd, specific number with a stated baseline — that's what makes something read as evidence instead of a pitch (source: Josh Braun, LinkedIn post, 31.12.2025, https://www.linkedin.com/posts/josh-braun_funny-how-numbers-work-in-sales-heres-share-7412100544950845440-nivC/).

## 2. Where Experts Disagree

**Disagreement 1 — how often to post.**
Lara Acosta's "4-3-2-1" framework treats 4 posts a week as non-negotiable. Her argument is quality gets rewarded, but you still need that floor for anything to compound (source: Lara Acosta, LinkedIn post, 28.05.2026, https://www.linkedin.com/posts/laraacostar_i-grew-to-340k-followers-on-linkedin-by-posting-ugcPost-7465716158055272448-m1of/).

Jasmin Alic went the other way. He started at 4-5 posts a week, dropped to 3-4, and now runs 2-3. He actually tested 6 posts a week for two weeks and pulled back because it wasn't sustainable if quality mattered to him (source: Jasmin Alic, LinkedIn post, 29.06.2026, https://www.linkedin.com/posts/alicjasmin_im-often-asked-about-my-linkedin-early-share-7477307230107754496-zNNR/).

I'd go with Jasmin Alic here. I'd rather post two or three times a week consistently than force out a fourth post just to hit a number — consistency beats motivation. Lara's cadence works because content is her whole business. For a founder running a brand alongside everything else, the pace has to be one you can actually keep up with, not one that looks better on paper.

**Disagreement 2 — how much to bet on video right now.**
Guillaume Moubeche tells B2B pages to skip video entirely and lean into text with an actual voice, based on what he saw auditing company pages (source: Guillaume Moubeche, "Grow your LinkedIn Business Page — My #1 Strategy," YouTube).

Jasmin Alic's numbers still favor text overall, but he's flagging that LinkedIn's newer video feed is currently giving niche-topic videos outsized reach — enough that he's adding video to his own strategy for the first time ever (source: Jasmin Alic, "2025 LinkedIn Strategies That Actually Work," YouTube).

I'd side with Jasmin here, partly because his read is the more recent one. But I agree with it on the substance too — niche topic matters. A new format can get pushed hard and reach a spike before it fades, while a consistent format keeps giving results you can actually expect. Richard van der Blom's data backs this up — the same push-then-fade pattern already happened with carousels and polls.

**Disagreement 3 — does the sales ask go in the post or stay out of it.**
Jasmin Alic never writes a sales post. The ask lives in a pinned comment under an educational post. His reasoning: putting the sell inside the post retroactively makes the whole thing feel like bait (source: Jasmin Alic, "2025 LinkedIn Strategies That Actually Work," YouTube).

Matt Lakajev's best-performing format does the opposite — story, lesson, result, then a direct "work with me" CTA baked right into the post (source: Matt Lakajev, LinkedIn post, 17.06.2025, https://www.linkedin.com/posts/mattlakajev_if-youre-trying-to-sell-on-linkedin-you-share-7340686459739193345-V8RT/).

I don't think these two are actually solving the same problem, so I wouldn't pick one and throw out the other. I'd focus on what the post itself is for. If it's a sales post, I wouldn't hesitate to bake a CTA right into it. But I'd be careful not to overdo it — turning every post into a sales pitch with a CTA attached. A few is fine. Not all of them.

## 3. What I Rejected and Why

**Matt Lakajev's 10-DMs-a-day model.** I actually think this approach is right — you won't find opportunities unless you go looking for them, and cold DMs done with respect instead of spam are a fair way to build a pipeline. But that's an outbound lead-gen tactic, not a content strategy (source: Matt Lakajev, LinkedIn post, 18.05.2026, https://www.linkedin.com/posts/mattlakajev_this-is-it-everything-learned-making-6m-share-7462013910561366016-010O/). This playbook is scoped to organic content, not outreach, so 10 DMs a day belongs in a different playbook — not because it's a bad tactic, but because it's answering a different question than the one this document is trying to answer.

**Guillaume Moubeche's curation strategy** — reposting and commenting on other creators' content to piggyback reach. It doesn't create a unique identity. If you're leaving the same kind of generic comment on everyone's posts, you're not actually saying anything — you're just spending time to look active. That's different from engaging with an actual question or a real point someone made, which is worth doing. But curation as a growth tactic, where the comment itself is interchangeable from one post to the next, is just wasted time dressed up as strategy (source: Guillaume Moubeche, "Grow your LinkedIn Business Page — My #1 Strategy," YouTube).

## 4. My Original Idea

**Keep a disagreement log next to the content calendar.**
None of the 10 sources suggested this. The sources contradict each other on cadence, format, and where the ask goes, so instead of picking one side per disagreement and locking it in forever, I'd log which expert's approach a given post followed, then track its Dark Social Score and save count against it. Run that for 8-12 weeks and the disagreements in Section 2 stop being a bet on someone else's data and start being an actual answer for this specific audience.

Why it could work: every number in this playbook comes from someone else's audience and someone else's niche. The fastest way to find out what actually transfers is to test it against your own numbers using the two measurement tools already in this playbook, instead of committing to one expert's opinion indefinitely.

## 5. Weaknesses of This Playbook

Every stat in here is self-reported by the person whose strategy it's backing up. None of the reach percentages, DM conversion numbers, or ARR figures are independently audited. That's a real gap I couldn't close with what's in this repo.

The B2B SaaS framing is inherited, not tested. Jasmin Alic, Lara Acosta, and Matt Lakajev built most of their methodology on personal-brand or coaching audiences, not enterprise SaaS buyers specifically. I'm assuming that transfers. It might not.

The video and carousel guidance is time-sensitive. Richard van der Blom's carousel data and Jasmin Alic's video-feed observation are both from mid-2026. If the algorithm shifts again, my call in Disagreement 2 has a short shelf life.

None of the sources address follower count as a variable. Advice from a 300k+ follower account and advice from a much smaller one probably don't transfer at the same rate, and nobody in this research controlled for that.

## 6. Who I Would NOT Recommend and Why

**Chris Orlob and Josh Braun.** Both are legit, well-sourced experts, just not on LinkedIn content strategy specifically — their material is sales calls and outreach mechanics. I pulled one useful adjacent data point from Josh Braun (the messaging-credibility point on specific numbers). Chris Orlob's material didn't end up supporting anything in this final playbook. Calling either of them core sources for a LinkedIn content strategy playbook overstates how directly their work applies here. Good for a sales-messaging playbook. Not this one.

**Samantha McKenna, with a caveat.** Her Dark Social Score is genuinely useful and I used it above, but most of what I collected from her is sales-conversation and email-rewrite coaching, not LinkedIn strategy. I'd recommend her narrowly for the one metric, not as a general source on this topic.

**Obaid Durrani.** Strong, verifiable results (scaled a content company from $1.7M to $7.2M ARR using LinkedIn organic), but everything I collected from him is about B2B influencer marketing and partnership structure — not the personal/brand content strategy this playbook covers. Different topic wearing the same "LinkedIn organic" label.
